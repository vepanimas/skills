# Tone and context guidance

Read this reference for sensitive messages, disagreement, escalation, boundaries, tone checks,
passive aggression, or product framing.

## Read the situation

Consider:

- channel and whether the exchange is public or private;
- audience and working relationship;
- purpose: inform, question, disagree, recommend, request, refuse, escalate, or document;
- stakes, urgency, and current level of tension;
- whether the author is exploring, recommending, deciding, or setting a boundary.

## Adapt by tendency, not template

- Keep Slack conversational and compact. Do not add greetings, headers, or sign-offs unless the
  draft already needs them.
- Keep issue entries observable and actionable. Preserve reproduction steps, expected and actual
  behavior, technical details, and open questions.
- Keep issue and PR comments focused on the specific behavior or decision under discussion.
- Preserve the author's structure in PR descriptions, design documents, and RFCs. Improve ordering
  only when requested or when the existing order obscures the point.
- In leadership communication, make the decision, ask, consequence, or outcome easy to find when it
  is already present. Do not invent a business rationale.
- In email, add a subject, greeting, or sign-off only when the user requests a complete email or the
  supplied draft clearly includes those elements.

## Make disagreement professional without weakening it

Remove judgments about intelligence, competence, motives, status, or character. Translate them into
the concrete disagreement only when that disagreement is supported by the draft.

Prefer the relevant parts of this shape when useful:

> observation → concern or impact → position → request or next step

Do not force every message into the full sequence. Do not invent a missing impact, proposal, or next
step.

## Remove passive aggression in both directions

Watch for:

- scorekeeping such as "as I already said" or "per my last message";
- fake uncertainty such as "correct me if I'm wrong, but" when the author is not genuinely unsure;
- veiled judgment such as "interesting choice" or "I'm surprised that";
- false warmth, smileys, or exclamation marks attached to criticism;
- excessive apologies or self-minimization around a legitimate request;
- decisions disguised as open questions;
- `we` used to obscure who owns the work or decision.

Replace these with the direct concern or ask already present. Preserve genuine uncertainty, optional
requests, and real warmth. Do not add a deadline, owner, reason, or consequence merely to make the
message more actionable.

Repetition can be evidence without becoming a jab. Keep meaningful counts and the author's role, but
focus on the unresolved disagreement:

> "I've explained the use cases twice, and we still seem to be working from different assumptions
> about what is in scope."

Add a question or next step only if the author supplied or clearly implied one.

## Preserve boundaries and escalation

Courtesy and firmness are compatible. Keep a refusal, requirement, deadline, or approval condition
clear. Remove personal attacks, not the boundary itself.

If the message is likely to escalate conflict in a public channel, briefly suggest a DM, call, or
follow-up document, but still provide a usable rewrite for the user to judge.

## Product framing

For product or leadership contexts, clarify an existing connection to user impact, developer
experience, cost, speed, quality, maintainability, risk, or tradeoffs. Do not add one simply because
it would make the message sound more strategic. Engineer-to-engineer comments should remain
technical when product framing would not help.
